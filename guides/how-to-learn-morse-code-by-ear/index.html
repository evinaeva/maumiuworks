To learn Morse code by ear, listen to each character as a whole sound at a real speed, usually 15 to 20 words per minute, instead of memorizing dots and dashes from a chart. Start with just two characters, add one more each time you copy about 90% correctly (the Koch method), and use extra space between characters (Farnsworth timing) so you have time to think while the characters themselves stay fast. Practice a little every day rather than a lot once a week: the CW Academy beginner course, for example, covers all the letters and digits in its first five weeks with daily practice.

Why shouldn't I learn Morse code from a chart?

A chart teaches you to see "dah-di-dah-dah" and count it. Your ear then has to translate sound into a picture, count the parts and look up the letter. That works at very slow speeds and then stops working, because there is no time to count.

At 20 words per minute, the letter Y (dah-di-dah-dah) lasts 13 timing units of 60 milliseconds each, which is 0.78 seconds including the gaps inside the letter. You cannot count four elements and search a mental table in that time, but you can recognize a familiar sound, the way you recognize a spoken word. This is why CW clubs teach by sound. The Long Island CW Club says plainly that it does not teach characters as visual strings of dots and dashes; the goal is to recognize each character as a complete sound pattern.

How does Morse code timing work?

Everything in Morse is measured in one unit, the length of a dit:

ElementLength
Dit1 unit
Dah3 units
Gap inside a character1 unit
Gap between characters3 units
Gap between words7 units

Speed is defined with the word "PARIS", which is exactly 50 units long including the space after it. One word per minute means PARIS fits 1 time per minute, so the length of a dit in milliseconds is 1200 divided by the speed in WPM. At 20 WPM a dit is 60 ms and a dah is 180 ms; at 5 WPM a dit is 240 ms.

What is the Koch method?

The Koch method was published in 1936 by the German researcher Ludwig Koch. It has two rules:

  1. Characters are always sent at the full target speed, from the first lesson.
  2. You start with only two characters. When you can copy random groups of them with about 90% accuracy, you add one more, and you repeat until you know the whole set.

Because the characters are fast from day one, you never get a chance to count them, so you build direct sound recognition. Each new character makes your accuracy dip for a while, then it recovers; that dip is normal and is not a sign to slow the characters down. Many trainers use a fixed Koch order that starts with K and M.

What is Farnsworth timing, and what is the difference between character speed and effective speed?

Character speed is how fast each letter is sent. Effective speed (also called overall or Farnsworth speed) is how many words per minute you get once the gaps are included. Farnsworth timing keeps the characters at their full speed and stretches only the gaps between characters and words.

The math, as used by ARRL's timing standard by Jon Bloom (KE3Z): of the 50 units in PARIS, 31 are inside the characters and stay at character speed; the other 19 (four character gaps of 3 units and one word gap of 7) are stretched until the whole word takes 60 divided by the effective speed in seconds.

You hear the same crisp letter shapes, with more thinking time in between. As you improve, shrink the gaps until the effective speed matches the character speed. The CW Academy of the CWops club, for example, starts beginners at 20 WPM characters with extra spacing, and notes that a beginner may finish its first course at around 5 WPM effective speed.

How long does it take to learn Morse code?

There is no fixed time, because practice habits matter more than talent. Some real reference points:

  • The CW Academy beginner course runs about eight weeks, with two classes a week. Students learn all 26 letters, 10 digits, some punctuation and Q codes in the first five weeks, and are asked to practice 45 to 60 minutes every day.
  • The same manual stresses that regular, consistent practice is the key, and that a long session every third day does not replace daily practice.
  • The Long Island CW Club has no fixed course length: students move up when they are ready.

As a rough guide, not a promise: with daily practice, expect the alphabet and digits within one to two months, and comfortable copying of a simple contact (call sign, signal report, name, location) some months after that. Speed comes later and can keep growing for years.

What does a good daily practice plan look like?

Keep sessions short and frequent. Consistency is what the courses above stress, and a 15 to 20 minute block is easy to fit in once or twice a day. Here is one way to use it:

MinutesWhat to do
2Warm up: listen to the characters you already know, no writing
8 to 10Copy random groups of your current Koch set; check your accuracy
3 to 5If you are at 90% or higher over enough answers, add the next character and copy it mixed with the rest
3Copy real words made only of letters you know
2Send a few characters on a key, or tap them, and listen to your own rhythm

Some practical tips:

  • Do not slow the characters down when it gets hard. Add more Farnsworth spacing instead.
  • Do not write dots and dashes. Write the letter, or say it in your head, then move on. If you miss one, let it go and catch the next.
  • Practice your weak characters. Most learners mix up pairs that sound alike, such as U and V or D and B.
  • Use a pleasant tone. Pick a pitch you can listen to for a long time; many operators like something between about 500 and 800 Hz.
  • Add head copy later. Once you know the letters, listen to words without writing them down.

Do I need Morse code for the ham radio exam?

No, not in the United States. The FCC removed the Morse code exam for the General and Amateur Extra licenses with a rule effective February 23, 2007; the Technician license had already had no code test. Many hams still learn CW because it is a satisfying way to operate and gets through when voice does not, but it is now a choice, not a license requirement. Rules in other countries vary, so check with your national licensing authority.

Questions and answers

What is the fastest way to learn Morse code?

Learn by sound at real character speed using the Koch method: start with two characters, add one each time you copy about 90% correctly, and practice a little every day. Use Farnsworth spacing for thinking time instead of slowing the characters down.

What speed should I learn Morse code at?

Most clubs and trainers use a character speed of 15 to 20 WPM from the start, with extra spacing between characters so the effective speed is lower. Learning characters at 5 WPM tends to teach counting, which has to be unlearned later.

How long does it take to learn Morse code by ear?

With daily practice, many learners know all the letters and digits within one to two months. The CW Academy beginner course covers 26 letters, 10 digits and some punctuation in its first five weeks of an eight-week course.

What is the difference between the Koch and Farnsworth methods?

Koch is about which characters you learn and when: two at first, then one more at 90% accuracy. Farnsworth is about timing: characters at full speed with longer gaps between them. Most modern courses combine both.

Do I still need Morse code for an amateur radio license?

Not in the United States: the FCC dropped the last Morse code exam requirement (for General and Extra) in 2007, so no US license class needs it. Many operators learn CW anyway to use it on the air.
