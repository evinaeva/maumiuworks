For a single-phase or DC circuit, voltage drop is VD = 2 × K × I × L ÷ CM, where K is the conductor's resistance constant (about 12.9 for copper and 21.2 for aluminum at 75 °C), I is the current in amps, L is the one-way length in feet and CM is the conductor area in circular mils. For a three-phase circuit, replace the 2 with 1.732 (√3). Divide the result by the circuit voltage to get percent: the NEC's informational notes recommend about 3% for a branch circuit and 5% for feeder plus branch combined, but in most cases these are recommendations, not enforceable requirements.

Safety note: this guide explains the arithmetic; it is not design advice. Your local code, the authority having jurisdiction and a licensed electrician decide what is acceptable for a real installation.

What is voltage drop and why does it matter?

Every conductor has resistance, so current loses some voltage along the way: the longer the run, the higher the current and the smaller the wire, the bigger the loss. Too much drop means motors that start hard and run hot, lights that dim and electronics that misbehave, and the lost voltage becomes heat in the wire.

Voltage drop does not replace ampacity. Size the wire for the current it must carry safely first, then check the drop and upsize if needed.

What are the voltage drop formulas?

CircuitFormula with KFormula with resistance per 1,000 ft
DC or single-phase, 2 wiresVD = 2 × K × I × L ÷ CMVD = 2 × I × L × R ÷ 1,000
Three-phase, balancedVD = 1.732 × K × I × L ÷ CMVD = 1.732 × I × L × R ÷ 1,000
  • VD is the voltage drop in volts.
  • K is the resistance of a conductor 1 circular mil in area and 1 foot long, in ohms. For uncoated copper at 75 °C it is about 12.9; for aluminum it is about 21.2.
  • I is the load current in amps.
  • L is the one-way length of the circuit in feet. The factor 2 already accounts for the current going out and coming back, so do not double the length yourself.
  • CM is the conductor area in circular mils, from NEC Chapter 9, Table 8.
  • R is the conductor's resistance in ohms per 1,000 feet, also from Chapter 9, Table 8.
  • Percent drop = VD ÷ circuit voltage × 100. For three-phase, use the line-to-line voltage.

You can rearrange the same formula to find the smallest wire that meets a limit: CM = 2 × K × I × L ÷ VD allowed (or 1.732 instead of 2 for three-phase).

Where do K = 12.9 and K = 21.2 come from?

K is simply Table 8's resistance multiplied by the conductor's area. For example, 1 AWG copper has an area of 83,690 circular mils and a resistance of 0.154 ohms per 1,000 feet at 75 °C, so K = 0.154 ÷ 1,000 × 83,690 ≈ 12.9. Aluminum has higher resistance, so its K is about 21.2.

Worked example: single-phase 120 V circuit

A 120 V circuit feeds a 16 A load 100 feet away. Will 12 AWG copper stay within 3%?

  1. VD = 2 × 12.9 × 16 × 100 ÷ 6,530 = 41,280 ÷ 6,530 = 6.32 V.
  2. Percent = 6.32 ÷ 120 × 100 = 5.27%. That is well over 3%.
  3. Smallest area for 3%: the allowed drop is 3% of 120 V = 3.6 V, so CM = 41,280 ÷ 3.6 = 11,467 circular mils.
  4. 10 AWG (10,380 circular mils) is too small: 41,280 ÷ 10,380 = 3.98 V, or 3.31%.
  5. 8 AWG (16,510 circular mils) works: 41,280 ÷ 16,510 = 2.50 V, or 2.08%.

Check with resistance instead: 2 × 16 × 100 × 1.98 ÷ 1,000 = 6.34 V, practically the same as step 1.

The same 16 A load at 240 V on 12 AWG drops the same 6.32 V, but that is only 2.63% of 240 V. Higher voltage is one of the most effective ways to reduce percent drop on long runs.

How far can 12 AWG copper go at 16 A and 120 V within 3%? L = VD × CM ÷ (2 × K × I) = 3.6 × 6,530 ÷ (2 × 12.9 × 16) = about 57 feet.

Worked example: three-phase 480 V feeder

A 480 V three-phase feeder carries 50 A over 200 feet in 6 AWG copper.

  1. VD = 1.732 × 12.9 × 50 × 200 ÷ 26,240 = 223,428 ÷ 26,240 = 8.51 V.
  2. Percent = 8.51 ÷ 480 × 100 = 1.77%.

In 4 AWG aluminum (41,740 circular mils) the same feeder gives 1.732 × 21.2 × 50 × 200 ÷ 41,740 = 8.80 V, or 1.83%. Ampacity, terminations and the rest of the circuit still have to be checked separately.

What does the NEC actually require?

The familiar 3% and 5% figures come from Informational Notes in NEC 210.19 (branch circuits) and 215.2 (feeders): about 3% at the farthest outlet of a branch circuit, and no more than 5% for feeder and branch circuit combined, as a level that gives reasonable efficiency of operation. The note numbers have moved between code editions. Informational notes are explanatory material, not mandatory requirements, so the NEC does not make 3% or 5% enforceable for ordinary circuits.

There are exceptions. A few articles make voltage drop mandatory, for example 647.4(D) for sensitive electronic equipment and 695.7 for fire pumps. Equipment installation instructions, which 110.3(B) requires you to follow, can set minimum voltages too. Some jurisdictions also amend the code to turn the recommendation into a rule, so check your local adoption.

How do I calculate voltage drop in mm² (IEC method)?

Outside North America, wire is sized in mm² and the reference is IEC 60364-5-52. Its informative Annex G gives:

u = b × (ρ1 × L ÷ S × cos φ + λ × L × sin φ) × IB

  • u is the voltage drop in volts, IB the design current in amps, L the length in meters and S the cross-section in mm².
  • b is 2 for single-phase circuits and 1 for balanced three-phase circuits. With b = 1, u is the drop per phase, so the percent is taken against the line-to-neutral voltage U0.
  • ρ1 is the resistivity in normal service, taken as 1.25 times the resistivity at 20 °C: 0.0225 Ω·mm²/m for copper and 0.036 Ω·mm²/m for aluminum.
  • λ is the reactance per unit length. The reactance term is small for small cables and is often left out for final circuits; for large cables use the manufacturer's data.

Example: a 230 V single-phase circuit, 16 A resistive load (cos φ = 1), 30 m of 2.5 mm² copper:

  1. u = 2 × 0.0225 × 30 ÷ 2.5 × 16 = 8.64 V.
  2. Percent = 8.64 ÷ 230 × 100 = 3.76%.
  3. With 4 mm²: u = 2 × 0.0225 × 30 ÷ 4 × 16 = 5.40 V, or 2.35%.

The UK's BS 7671 tables express the same idea in millivolts per amp per meter: 2.5 mm² twin-and-earth is listed at 18 mV/A/m, and 18 × 16 × 30 ÷ 1,000 = 8.64 V, the same result.

Three-phase example: 400 V, 32 A, cos φ = 0.85, 50 m of 6 mm² copper, reactance ignored: u = 1 × 0.0225 × 50 ÷ 6 × 0.85 × 32 = 5.10 V per phase, which is 2.21% of the phase voltage U0 = 400/√3 ≈ 231 V (and equals 8.83 V, or 2.21%, line to line).

Annex G's Table G.52.1 suggests limits of 3% for lighting and 5% for other uses in installations supplied directly from a public low-voltage network, and 6% and 8% for installations with their own private supply. Like the NEC notes, this annex is informative; national wiring rules decide what is required.

What mistakes make voltage drop results wrong?

  • Doubling the length twice. Use one-way length with the factor 2 (or 1.732), never round-trip length with it.
  • Using the wrong current. Use the actual load current, not the breaker rating, unless you want a worst-case check.
  • Taking percent of the wrong voltage. For three-phase VD with 1.732, divide by the line-to-line voltage.
  • Ignoring reactance on big conductors. Long AC runs with large wire read slightly low with K or Table 8; use Table 9 impedance for those.
  • Forgetting it is a check, not a size. Ampacity, terminal ratings and overcurrent protection come first.

To reduce drop on a long run, use a larger conductor, a higher circuit voltage if the load allows it, a shorter route, or split the load across circuits.

Questions and answers

What is the formula for voltage drop?

For single-phase or DC circuits, VD = 2 × K × I × L ÷ CM, with K about 12.9 for copper and 21.2 for aluminum, L the one-way length in feet and CM the area in circular mils. For three-phase circuits, use 1.732 instead of 2.

Is 3% voltage drop required by the NEC?

Not for ordinary circuits. The 3% branch and 5% combined figures are in informational notes, which are recommendations. A few articles, such as 647.4(D) and 695.7, do set mandatory limits, and local amendments may add more.

What is the K value for copper and aluminum?

About 12.9 ohms per circular mil-foot for uncoated copper and 21.2 for aluminum, both at 75 °C. They come from NEC Chapter 9, Table 8, by multiplying a conductor's resistance by its area.

Is length in the voltage drop formula one-way or round trip?

One-way. The factor 2 in the single-phase formula already covers the return conductor, and the 1.732 factor in the three-phase formula accounts for the phase relationship.
