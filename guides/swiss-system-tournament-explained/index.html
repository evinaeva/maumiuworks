A Swiss system tournament is a fixed number of rounds in which, each round, players are paired against opponents with the same or a similar score whom they have not played before. Nobody is eliminated, everyone plays every round, and the winner is the player with the most points at the end. You need at least log2 N rounds, rounded up, to separate a single leader: 5 rounds for 32 players, 7 for 128, instead of the 31 or 127 rounds a full round robin would take.

How does Swiss pairing work?

The rules most organizers follow come from chess, where FIDE (the International Chess Federation) publishes the basic rules for Swiss systems:

  • Two players never meet twice. No rematches in the whole event.
  • Players are paired with others on the same score. Players on the same number of points form a score group, and pairing happens inside the group whenever possible.
  • If a score group has an odd number of players, one player "floats" to the next group down and plays someone with a slightly lower score.
  • Colors (or home and away) are balanced. In chess, a player should not get the same color three times in a row, and the number of White and Black games should stay close.
  • The pairing for the next round is made only after the current round is finished, because it depends on the results.

A common way to pair inside a score group (the basis of the Dutch system used in most rated chess events) is to sort the group by rating or seed and pair the top half against the bottom half: in a group of 8, seed 1 plays 5, 2 plays 6, 3 plays 7 and 4 plays 8. Round 1 works the same way on the whole field.

The minimum is enough to find one clear leader if the top players keep winning. In practice draws and upsets spread the scores, so many events play one or two rounds more than the minimum to rank the places behind the winner more reliably. Large chess opens with hundreds of players often play 9 rounds, and the Chess Olympiad plays 11. Never schedule more rounds than N-1, because then pairings without rematches run out.

The total number of games is rounds × N/2, rounded down for an odd N.

How are byes handled in a Swiss tournament?

With an odd number of players, one player sits out each round and gets a "pairing bye". Under FIDE's basic Swiss rules:

  • The bye is worth the points of a win, unless the tournament rules set a different value, and every bye in the event is worth the same.
  • No player gets more than one bye (or more than one win's worth of points without playing).

In practice the pairing systems give the bye to a player at the bottom of the standings who has not had one yet, so the leaders are not handed a point without a game.

Some clubs give a half point for a bye instead. Either is acceptable; announce it before round 1. Byes requested by a player in advance (for example, to skip a round) are a separate thing and usually score zero or a half point.

What is the Buchholz tiebreak?

Ties are very common in Swiss events, because a few rounds cannot separate everyone. Buchholz is the most widely used tiebreak: the sum of the final scores of all your opponents. It measures how strong your opposition was. Two players on the same score are ranked by who scored their points against tougher opponents.

So the order is 2, 3, 4. Player 2 lost only to the winner and beat a 2-point player; player 4 picked up a point against the last-placed player.

What other Swiss tiebreaks are there?

  • Buchholz Cut 1: Buchholz without your lowest-scoring opponent. It reduces the effect of one very weak opponent.
  • Median Buchholz: Buchholz without both the highest- and lowest-scoring opponents.
  • Sonneborn-Berger: the sum of the scores of the opponents you beat, plus half the scores of those you drew with. It rewards beating strong players.
  • Direct encounter: if the tied players met, the result of that game.
  • Number of wins: favors decisive results over draws.
  • Cumulative (progressive) score: add up your running score after each round. A player who won early, and so faced stronger opponents later, ranks higher.
  • Average rating of opponents: common in rated chess events.
  • Opponents' match-win percentage: the main tiebreak in Magic: The Gathering events, where each opponent's percentage is counted as at least 33%.

FIDE's tie-break regulations also include special rules for rounds a player did not play, so that a bye or a forfeit does not unfairly push anyone's Buchholz up or down. Whatever you use, choose the list before the event and publish it.

When is Swiss better than a round robin?

Swiss is the better choice when:

  • You have more players than you have rounds. 30 players and an evening for 5 rounds rules out a round robin (29 rounds).
  • You want nobody eliminated. Everyone plays every round, unlike in a knockout bracket.
  • Players arrive with different strengths. After a round or two the strong play the strong and the beginners get even games, which is more fun for everyone.

A round robin is better when the group is small (up to about 10), when every player must face every other player (a club championship, a qualifying group), or when you need an indisputable ranking of every place. Swiss ranks the top places well but the middle of the table depends more on tiebreaks.

What are the downsides of the Swiss system?

  • Pairing by hand is error-prone. Score groups, floaters, colors and the no-rematch rule interact, especially in the last rounds. Use software.
  • You cannot publish the whole schedule in advance. Each round is paired from the previous round's results.
  • Ties are common, so tiebreaks decide a lot of places.
  • Late in the event the leader may already be decided while others still play for places.

Questions and answers

How many rounds is a Swiss tournament with 16 players?

At least 4 rounds, because 2 to the power of 4 is 16. Many organizers play 5 to rank the places behind the winner better. Each round has 8 games.

Can you play the same opponent twice in a Swiss tournament?

No. The core rule of the Swiss system is that two players never meet more than once in the same event.

What does Buchholz mean in chess?

Buchholz is a tiebreak: the sum of the final scores of all the opponents a player faced. Among players on the same score, the one with the higher Buchholz had the tougher opponents and ranks higher.

Is a bye a win in a Swiss tournament?

Under FIDE's basic rules a pairing bye is worth the points of a win unless the tournament rules say otherwise, and each player can get it only once. Some clubs score it as a half point instead.

What is the difference between Swiss and round robin?

In a round robin everyone plays everyone, which takes N-1 rounds. In Swiss you play only a few rounds, about log2 N, against opponents on a similar score, so it handles large fields in far less time.
