Good livestock health records give every animal a unique ID and log every treatment the moment it is given: the product name and batch number, the dose, the date, the animals treated, who gave it, and the meat and milk withdrawal periods with the date each one ends. Add births, weights, purchases, sales and deaths to the same animal's history, and keep the records long enough for your rules: in the UK and the EU, keepers of food-producing animals must keep medicine records for at least five years. The main reasons are food safety (no drug residues in milk or meat), traceability and better herd decisions.

This guide is general information for cattle, goat and sheep keepers, not legal or veterinary advice. Your national rules, your milk buyer or assurance scheme and your veterinarian set what you must record.

Why keep livestock health records?

  • To avoid drug residues in food. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration explains that adequate records lower the risk of illegal drug residues: with them, you know which animals were treated, with what, how and when, and when the withdrawal period ends, so animals are sent to slaughter only after drug residues have dropped below the tolerance.
  • Because the law often requires it. Many countries require keepers of food-producing animals to record the medicines they use and to show those records on inspection.
  • For traceability. If there is a disease outbreak, a residue finding or a question from a buyer, you can show what happened to each animal and when.
  • For better decisions. Treatment history shows which animals keep getting sick, which products work, and when to cull or change management. Weights and birth records show which lines grow and breed best.

What should a livestock health record include?

Think of two layers: a card for each animal, and an entry for each event on that card.

For each animal:

  • Unique ID: ear tag or official ID, plus any management tag, name or electronic ID, and old tag numbers if the animal was re-tagged
  • Species, breed, sex and birth date
  • Mother and father, if known, and where the animal came from (born on the farm or bought, from whom and when)
  • Group or pen
  • Status: present, sold, died, with the date

For each treatment or vaccination:

  • Date (and time, for multi-dose treatments)
  • Animal ID, or the group and the number of animals treated
  • Product name, and the active ingredient if the product is not well known
  • Batch or lot number
  • Dose, route (for example under the skin, into the muscle, oral or pour-on) and injection site
  • Reason for treatment, or the diagnosis
  • Who gave it, and the prescribing veterinarian if there was a prescription
  • Withdrawal period for meat and for milk, even if it is zero, and the date each ends
  • Next dose, if the course is not finished

Other events worth recording: births (with each newborn's ID), weights, breeding and pregnancy checks, weaning, hoof trimming, deworming, milk records, purchases, sales and deaths.

What do the rules say must be recorded?

Rules differ by country. Some examples, so you can see how close they are:

  • In the UK, the Veterinary Medicines Regulations 2013, regulation 19, require the keeper of food-producing animals to record, when buying a medicine, its name and batch number, the date, the quantity and the supplier's name and address; and when giving it, the product name, the date, the quantity given, the withdrawal period and the identification of the animals treated. UK government guidance says these records must be kept for at least five years, may be kept electronically, and must be available for inspection.
  • In the EU, Regulation (EU) 2019/6, Article 108, requires owners or keepers of food-producing animals to record the date of the first administration, the product name, the quantity given, the supplier, evidence of acquisition, the animal or group treated, the prescribing veterinarian if applicable, the withdrawal period even if it is zero, and the duration of treatment. The records must be kept for at least five years.
  • In the US, the FDA encourages all producers to keep treatment records with the drug name, the identity of the animal, the date of each administration, the total dose, how the drug was given, who gave it, the length of the withdrawal period and the date it ends. When a veterinarian prescribes a drug for extra-label use in food animals, federal rules (21 CFR 530.5) require the veterinarian to keep records, including the withdrawal times, for 2 years or longer if other federal or state law requires it.

Your milk buyer, processor or farm assurance program may ask for more, and for longer.

How do I work out the withdrawal end date?

Take the date of the last dose and add the withdrawal period in days, following the way the label or your veterinarian counts it. For example, if the last injection is on March 3 and the meat withdrawal is 28 days, the earliest date for slaughter is March 31; with a 7-day milk withdrawal, milk can go into the tank again from March 10, if the label counts days that way.

For multi-dose courses, a common mistake is counting from the first dose. Withdrawal starts after the last one. If you are not sure how the label counts, or if the animal was treated off-label, ask your veterinarian and write their answer in the record.

How should I record weights and births?

Weights: record the date, the weight and the method (scale or weigh tape). The useful number is daily gain: (new weight − previous weight) ÷ days between weighings. A steer that goes from 210 kg to 250 kg in 50 days gains 40 ÷ 50 = 0.8 kg, or 800 g, per day.

Births: record the date, the dam's ID, the sire if known, the number born (twins and triplets each get their own ID), sex, birth weight if you weigh, any assistance needed, and the ID you give each newborn. Many countries require new calves to be tagged and registered within a set number of days, so check your national deadline.

Should I keep records on paper or on a phone?

Both work if they are complete, legible and made at the time. Paper is simple but easy to lose, hard to search and hard to total. A spreadsheet is searchable but awkward in the barn. A phone app can be filled in next to the animal, can scan tags and can remind you when withdrawal periods end. Whatever you choose, back it up somewhere other than the device it lives on, and make sure you can export or print the records for an inspection.

Questions and answers

How long should I keep livestock medicine records?

In the UK and the EU, keepers of food-producing animals must keep medicine records for at least five years. In the US, veterinarians must keep extra-label prescription records for at least 2 years. What producers must keep, and for how long, depends on the program you are in, such as your state, milk buyer, processor or certifier, so ask them and keep at least what they require.

What is a withdrawal period?

It is the minimum time between the last dose of a medicine and the moment the animal's meat or milk can enter the food chain. It is printed on the label, may be different for meat and milk, and can be longer when a veterinarian prescribes a product off-label.

Do I need to record the batch number of a vaccine or medicine?

In the UK, keepers must record the batch number when they buy or receive a medicine for food-producing animals. Even where it is not required, it is good practice, because it lets you trace a problem back to a specific lot.

Can I keep livestock records on my phone?

Yes, as long as the records are complete, made at the time and can be shown or exported for an inspection. In the UK, government guidance says medicine records may be kept electronically.

What should I record when I treat a whole group of animals?

Record the date, the product and batch, the dose per animal and route, how many animals and which group or pen, who gave it and the withdrawal end dates. Under EU rules, a group can be identified as a group rather than animal by animal.
